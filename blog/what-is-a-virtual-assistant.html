What Is a Virtual Assistant? (And Why the Right One
Changes Everything)

Hannah — Verdant Virtual

Growth & Operations Partner · UK based

Published 15 January 2026 · Updated 26 February 2026

If you've been googling "virtual assistant" lately, you've probably landed on about a hundred different definitions — most of them either vague, slightly out of date, or written by someone who has clearly never actually been one.

So let me give you a straightforward answer, from someone who is.

My name is Hannah. I run Verdant Virtual, and I work as a growth and operations partner for founders and small business owners across the UK and the US. I've been in the executive support world for over eight years — across government, film production, and the creative industries — and the question I get asked most often, especially from people who've never worked with a VA before, is a simple one.

What exactly does a virtual assistant do?

It's a fair question. And the honest answer is: it depends entirely on who you hire.

The basic definition

A virtual assistant — usually shortened to VA — is a self employed professional who provides business support services remotely. They're not employed by you in the traditional sense. There's no office desk, no PAYE, no HR onboarding process. You work with them on a contract or retainer basis, and they handle a defined set of tasks or areas of your business from wherever they are in the world.

That last part matters more than people realise. Because "remote" doesn't mean disconnected. The best VAs operate as if they're embedded in your business — they just don't need to be in your building to do it.

What virtual assistants actually do

This is where it gets interesting, because the scope of what a VA can do has expanded enormously over the last decade. The old school image of a virtual assistant answering emails and booking flights still exists — and those services are genuinely valuable — but it's only a fraction of what the role can look like now.

Here's a more honest picture of what virtual assistants do in 2026:

Administrative and executive support. Managing inboxes, scheduling meetings, handling correspondence, booking travel, preparing documents, taking meeting notes, and everything else that keeps the operational wheels turning. This is the traditional core of VA work and it remains incredibly useful for founders who are drowning in their own inboxes.

Lead generation and sales support. Prospect research, outreach campaigns, CRM management, pipeline tracking, and follow up sequences. A good VA can own the entire top of funnel process, finding and warming leads so that by the time a prospect gets to you, the groundwork is already done.

Systems and operations. Workflow design, SOP creation, process documentation, automation setup, and tool configuration. This is the area I find most meaningful — building the infrastructure that lets a business run without its founder carrying every single piece of it.

Social media and content. Scheduling, caption writing, community management, hashtag research, and repurposing existing content across platforms. Not every VA offers this, but many do, and it's one of the areas where consistent support makes a visible difference quickly.

Project coordination. Keeping timelines on track, chasing deliverables, managing communication between multiple stakeholders, and making sure things don't fall through the gaps when everyone's busy.

Customer support. Handling inbound queries, managing support inboxes, responding to reviews, and making sure clients feel looked after without every conversation needing to go through the founder.

Why businesses hire virtual assistants

The reason people hire VAs has shifted over the years. It used to be primarily about cost — a VA was cheaper than a full time employee and could handle the overflow. That's still true, but it's no longer the main driver.

What most of my clients tell me when we first speak is some version of the same thing: I'm spending too much of my time on things that aren't my actual job.

They're the ones writing every email. Posting every social media update. Chasing every invoice. Coordinating every project. And in doing all of that, they're running out of time and energy for the strategic, creative, and relationship driven work that actually moves their business forward.

A virtual assistant solves a bandwidth problem — but when it's done well, it solves a focus problem too. The right VA doesn't just free up your hours. They free up your headspace. And that, in my experience, is what makes the real difference.

Virtual assistants in the UK and US: what to know

Whether you're based in the UK or the US, the VA market looks broadly similar — lots of options, a wide range of specialisations, and significant variation in quality and experience.

A few things worth knowing if you're comparing options across both markets:

Rates vary by location and expertise. UK based VAs and US based VAs are broadly comparable in terms of day rates, though there's a wide spectrum depending on experience level and specialisation. Don't automatically choose the cheapest option. The goal isn't to spend as little as possible — it's to find someone whose work generates more value than it costs.

Time zones are manageable. If you're a US based business working with a UK based VA (like me), the time difference is typically five to eight hours depending on where you are. In practice, most VA work is asynchronous anyway — and good communication structures mean this rarely causes problems.

Contracts and terms differ slightly. In the UK, IR35 and self employment rules mean VAs operate as independent contractors. In the US, the equivalent would be a 1099 arrangement. Either way, you're not taking on an employee — and that simplicity is part of the appeal.

What a virtual assistant is not

I think this is worth saying clearly, because there's a version of the VA industry that does the whole sector a disservice.

A virtual assistant is not someone who sits in a queue waiting for you to send them tasks and then completes those tasks without any context or ownership. That's a task execution service. It's useful for certain things, but it's very different from a genuine working partnership.

A good VA thinks. They ask questions before doing something wrong. They notice when a process isn't working and say something. They understand your business well enough to handle situations that weren't explicitly covered in a brief. They bring judgment, not just capacity.

That distinction matters enormously when you're choosing who to work with.

How virtual assistants compare to AI tools

This is the question I get asked most often right now, and I want to answer it properly rather than just dismissing it.

AI tools — ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and the rest — are genuinely impressive. I use them myself, as part of how I work. They're brilliant for certain things: drafting a first version of something, summarising a long document, generating options quickly, automating repetitive text tasks.

But there are things a founder actually needs that no AI tool has figured out yet.

An AI assistant doesn't know what "the situation with that client" means. It can't pick up on tone from a brief conversation and adjust how it handles a delicate follow up. It can't push back when you're about to send an email you'll regret. It has no institutional memory of your business, your relationships, or the decisions you made six months ago that are relevant to what's happening now.

More practically: AI tools are only as good as the prompts they receive. Which means someone still needs to know what to ask, how to frame it, and what to do with the output. In most small businesses, that person is still the founder — which means the "time saving" tool has just created a new kind of overhead.

A virtual assistant doesn't replace AI. A good one uses it. But the judgment, the context, the ownership, and the relationship — those are still deeply human, and they're still what makes the difference between getting things done and getting the right things done.

What to look for when you hire a virtual assistant

If you're thinking about working with a VA for the first time, a few things to look for:

Relevant experience. Not just years, but type. Has this person worked in environments where the stakes were real and the pace was demanding? Do they have a background that suggests they can operate independently rather than needing constant direction?

Clear communication. How they communicate in the process of you getting to know them tells you everything about how they'll communicate once you're working together. Vague, slow, or hard to follow answers in a first conversation tend to get worse, not better.

Genuine curiosity about your business. A VA who asks good questions about your business before talking about their services is one worth paying attention to. They're demonstrating that they think in terms of your needs, not their own offering.

Honest about fit. The best VAs I know are selective about who they work with — not because they're difficult, but because they understand that a bad fit wastes everyone's time and produces worse outcomes. If someone will work with literally anyone, that tells you something.

Is a virtual assistant right for you?

Not always. And I'd rather say that clearly than suggest a VA is the answer to every business problem.

A virtual assistant works best when the founder has a clear sense of what they want to delegate, or at least enough trust to work collaboratively with someone to figure it out. If you're at a stage where nothing is documented, nothing is repeatable, and you're not sure what you'd hand over — the first step might actually be building that clarity before bringing someone in.

That said, part of what a good operations focused VA does is help you get there. Building the systems, documenting the processes, and creating the structure that makes delegation possible in the first place.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a virtual assistant cost in the UK?

VA rates in the UK typically range from £25 to £60 per hour depending on experience and specialisation. Many VAs offer monthly retainers which can work out more cost effective if you need consistent ongoing support.

How much does a virtual assistant cost in the US?

US based VAs typically charge between $30 and $75 per hour, with similar variation based on experience level and the type of work involved. International VAs — including UK based ones — often offer competitive rates for US clients.

What's the difference between a virtual assistant and a personal assistant?

A personal assistant traditionally works in person, usually for one employer, and is employed rather than contracted. A virtual assistant works remotely, typically on a self employed basis, and often works with multiple clients simultaneously. The practical skills overlap significantly — the main differences are in structure and working arrangement.

Can a virtual assistant work across time zones?

Yes. Most VA work is asynchronous by nature — emails, tasks, projects, and admin don't require you to be online at the same time. With clear communication structures, time zone differences rarely create meaningful problems.

What tasks should I not give a virtual assistant?

Tasks that require physical presence are an obvious one. Beyond that, the things best kept in house are decisions that require deep company context that can't be transferred, anything involving legally sensitive authorisations, and strategic choices that are genuinely the founder's responsibility. Everything else is usually on the table.

How do I know if I'm ready to hire a virtual assistant?

If you're consistently running out of time, losing leads, missing follow ups, or spending your energy on operational tasks rather than growth, you're probably ready. The clearer you are about what's eating your time, the faster a good VA can start making a difference.
